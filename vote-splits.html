Vote Splits

How the same ballots voted across offices in Utah’s 2026 general election — from the complete statewide cast-vote record (29 of 29 counties, 1,058,810 ballots). Utah had no U.S. Senate race and no statewide executive race on the 2026 ballot, so the U.S. House contests are the top of the ticket; the analysis covers the 94 mapped partisan contests (4 U.S. House, 15 State Senate, 75 State House). Statistics built November 23, 2026.

Ballot profiles

Each ballot is classified by the partisan pattern of its own votes in the 94 key contests: straight Republican (at least one Republican vote, no Democratic or third-party vote), straight Democratic, mixed (Republican and Democratic votes, no third-party vote), third-party-involved (at least one third-party or independent vote — takes precedence over mixed), and no partisan vote. Undervotes and write-ins carry no partisan signal. A ballot with a single partisan vote is classified from that vote alone, which inflates the straight-ticket shares; the two-or-more-votes variant below is the better measure of splitting behavior.

Close contests and third-party votes

For each contest, the tables compare the winner’s margin with the votes cast for third-party and independent candidates. A contest is flagged “arithmetically spoiler-possible” when third-party votes exceed the margin. That is a screen, not a finding that spoiling occurred: it assumes third-party voters would otherwise have voted for a major-party candidate, and survey research finds most would instead have abstained (Devine & Kopko 2021). A second tier, near-spoilers, covers contested races where third-party votes reached at least half the margin without exceeding it; the same screen-not-finding caveat applies. The shape of a flag matters — in several flagged contests the third-party or independent candidate won or came second, which is a strong independent candidacy, not a trailing spoiler.
Reading the tables. Two tiers. Flagged contests are those where third-party votes exceeded the winner’s margin. Near-spoilers are contested races where third-party votes reached at least 50% of the margin but did not exceed it — the next-closest contest sits at 38% of its margin, so the 50% line is a break in the data, not a chosen cutoff. “Best third-party finish” shows where the strongest third-party or independent candidate placed. Only contests where that finish is third or lower have the classic spoiler shape.

Methods & sources

Data. County Disclosure Controlled cast-vote records published through the state results site, all 29 counties, 1,058,810 ballots. Candidate parties come from the official state results file; all 210 candidates in the 94 key contests are party-mapped. The cast-vote-record total is 3 ballots below the certified statewide total of 1,058,813 — a known feed undercount localized to three candidates (U.S. House District 3: Maloy −2, Udell −1; State House District 69: Monson −1), not missing ballots. County and district figures are aggregates only; no ballot is assigned to a precinct, and no individual ballot data is published here.
Coverage. Key contests are the 94 mapped federal and state partisan contests: 4 U.S. House, 15 State Senate (only 15 of 29 Senate seats were on the 2026 ballot), and 75 State House. Excluded: State School Board (nonpartisan office — no party label on the ballot), constitutional amendments and judicial retentions (not candidate contests), and county and local contests (no authoritative party source; parties are never guessed). 799 ballots carried only a U.S. House contest (federal-only ballots) and appear in no State House or State Senate pair. Nine contests were uncontested (SD14, HD19, HD21, HD34, HD40, HD47, HD57, HD60, HD65) and have no margin, so they appear in neither tier of the close-contest tables.
What this page can and cannot show. The cast-vote record shows exactly how each ballot voted in each contest — splitting rates, crossover flows, and third-party vote totals are observed facts, not estimates. What it cannot show is what any voter would have done under a different set of choices: a pick-one ballot records a first choice only, so second choices and counterfactual outcomes are not in the data. The spoiler screen is arithmetic only; whether a third-party candidacy changed an outcome depends on how its voters would have behaved without it, which the record does not reveal.
Methods literature. The ballot-classification template follows Kuriwaki et al. (2024), “Cast vote records: A database of ballots from the 2020 U.S. Election,” Scientific Data 11(1), doi:10.1038/s41597-024-04017-1. The bounded-reallocation approach to third-party voters follows Herron & Lewis (2007), “Did Ralph Nader Spoil Al Gore’s Presidential Bid?,” Quarterly Journal of Political Science 2(3): 205–226. The abstention finding — that most third-party voters would have abstained rather than choose a major-party candidate — is from Devine & Kopko (2021), “Did Gary Johnson and Jill Stein Cost Hillary Clinton the Presidency?,” The Forum 19(2): 173–201, doi:10.1515/for-2021-0011.
Data downloads. The statistics behind this page, as JSON: ballot-profile-distribution.json, crosstabs.json, spoiler-arithmetic.json, third-party-profiles.json.
